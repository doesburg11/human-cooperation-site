## Definition

**The definition used on this site.** In the broad sense used here, kin selection means: *a helping trait spreads because its benefits go to individuals related to the helper by common descent, the helper's own offspring included, even though helping costs the helper some of its own survival or reproduction.* This is deliberately broad. In standard inclusive-fitness terms, benefits to one's own offspring count as *direct* fitness and benefits to other relatives as *indirect* fitness; this site groups both under one label and always says which one a result belongs to. A result counts as kin selection here only if all three of these hold:

1. **A real cost.** Counting any share of the benefit that comes back to the helper itself, helping leaves the helper with less survival or reproduction of its own than not helping. Helping that pays for itself (weak altruism, the case in the [Moran kin-selection runs](/evolved-cooperation/kin-selection#later-tests-self-benefit-and-speed)) does not count.
2. **The benefit goes to relatives by descent.** The recipients are the helper's offspring or other relatives by common descent, not the helper itself and not unrelated individuals who happen to be nearby. In these models inheritance is clonal, so offspring carry the parent's helping trait apart from mutation.
3. **Spread from rare, against a control.** Helping rises from a rare start to an endpoint set in advance, and a comparison registered in advance shows that it does not when the benefit no longer goes to relatives (or their relatedness is removed). Where that comparison changes several things at once, the result is reported as *consistent with* kin selection, not as proof that relatedness alone caused it.

These are the ingredients of a test of Hamilton's rule, $rB > C$: the relatedness-weighted benefit $rB$ exceeds the helper's net cost $C$. The broad definition has two sub-cases, and every result on this site says which one it belongs to:

- **Kin selection through parental care:** the recipients are the helper's own offspring.
- **Kin selection among non-offspring relatives:** the recipients are siblings, cousins or neighbours who share descent. This is the classic indirect-fitness case at the centre of Hamilton's theory. Much of the literature (for example West, Griffin and Gardner 2007) keeps the name "kin selection" for this sub-case alone and treats care for one's own offspring as direct fitness. This site uses the broad definition.

**The goal.** To establish kin selection, in this broad sense, in an **ecological setting**: individuals with energy reserves, a limited renewable food supply, death by starvation, births that need resources and go into free space, and individuals that move in search of food, so that population size and empty space follow resources rather than a fixed replacement rule.

**Where it stands.** Each row is one step. The rows are grouped by how the population is regulated: in the first block, a Moran-style replacement rule keeps the population size fixed (every death is refilled at once by a neighbour's offspring, so the grid stays full); in the second, population size is not fixed and follows births, deaths and resources, as the goal requires. The Yes/No columns show which elements its model contains, and the last two columns say whether helping spread from rare and where the experiment is. Yes/No = the element is in the model or not; ✓ = achieved, ✗ = not achieved yet.

**Note.** Still to achieve: steps 6 to 9, and step 5 for care of own offspring and with energy and limited food. The transition from the first block to the second succeeded in a ported published model with empty-site islands, twice: help to patch-mates (2026-11-25) and help among non-offspring relatives, own offspring excluded (2026-11-26; see The Ladder in Detail); every attempt built on the site's own lattice failed. A restart of step 5 on the step-3 base, without energy (2026-11-24), also missed its endpoint and reached its agreed hard stop, which closes the route of rebuilding the Moran model step by step. An attempt to replicate a published model that already has goal A's ecology, Taylor-Davies et al. (2024), in which parents evolve to feed their own infants on a grid with energy, regrowing food, births into free tiles and movement, stopped before any test (2026-11-25): with the authors' public code, neither the settings stated in the paper (4 of 24 populations survived 10,000 steps) nor the code's default settings (3 of 24) kept populations alive, while the paper reports surviving populations in all its runs. The settings the authors actually used could not be recovered, so their feeding results were not tested here. No kin-selection experiment so far has agents that move in search of food: in steps 1 to 6 they stay on their site for life, step 7 has no space at all, and in step 8 a few adults make one random jump when they mature. A planned movement step on the ladder (R5b) was skipped because the grid was almost full. On the lattice, step 5 failed once births needed free space; a theory screen of Alizon and Taylor (2008) suggests a possible reason: births there were limited by free space next to the parent rather than by fecundity, which resembles the case in which their analysis says extra fecundity mostly competes with relatives (a hypothesis, not tested). One more rung is agreed before the kin line pauses (2026-11-26): care for own offspring in the island model; energy and limited food, then movement, would follow later, one ingredient at a time. Steps 7 to 9 are the harder track, help to relatives other than offspring; this track has now worked once at step 5, in the island model (2026-11-26). Before that, step 2 worked only at benefit-to-cost ratios of 12 and 24, and its control changes local settling and relatedness together; the ladder never added ecology to it; the ecological kin model (step 7) rose in some runs, but that could not be separated from the population's survival; and the ecological network model (step 8) has costly help with no return to the helper, but tracks no relatedness and showed only a very small rise. It could still be a starting point for this track. Two non-kin models already have more of the goal's ecology and could host either goal: [Cooperative Hunting](/evolved-cooperation/cooperative-hunting) has an energy budget, regrowing grass, starvation and agents that move, but returns the shared kill to the hunters and has no births into free space; the [Spatial Prisoner's Dilemma](/evolved-cooperation/spatial-prisoners-dilemma) has births into adjacent empty cells and some movement, but no limited food. Neither tracks relatedness.

## The Ladder in Detail

The [Moran kin-selection model](/evolved-cooperation/kin-selection) is a full grid where every site is refilled each step by a neighbour's offspring. An earlier [ecological kin-selection model](https://github.com/doesburg11/EvolvedCooperation/tree/main/ecological_models/nowak_mechanisms/kin_selection) (sexual reproduction, households, care for juveniles) let helping rise from rare in some runs (2 of 5 in its proof suite), also when relatedness cues were shuffled. But its controls did not separate that spread from population survival: populations persisted with helper care, while no tested survival floor (up to 0.4) kept populations without helpers alive. So it is not an established kin-selection result. In a follow-up, learned instead of inherited helping gave no detectable persistence benefit over a small constant care effort. A separate line of work, the [kin selection ladder](https://github.com/doesburg11/EvolvedCooperation/tree/main/ecological_models/nowak_mechanisms/kin_selection/ladder), asked whether kin selection survives as the model becomes more like a real ecology. Starting from the Moran model, it added one ingredient at a time (each pre-registered, with at most two attempts and its own control), and required helping to spread from rare at every step.

**What the ladder adds.** Kin selection among non-offspring relatives, with a real cost to the helper, did appear outside the simplest model, but in one step only, at the higher benefit-to-cost ratios tested (12 and 24), and only while offspring stayed near their parents. Kin selection through parental care was more robust across the tested variants, but in these tests it did not require offspring to stay near their parents. One late result points in an unexpected direction: once births went into free sites, care for own young spread much further when offspring dispersed across the whole grid (to near-maximum in 20/20 runs) than when they stayed local. That pattern is consistent with kin competition (relatives competing with each other for the same nearby space, which in theory can cancel the benefit of helping them; Taylor 1992), but it was not tested directly. A follow-up measurement on the same runs (diagnostic DR8, exploratory) looked at the neighbourhoods of would-be parents. With local births, about two thirds of breeding opportunities found no free neighbouring site, for strong and weak helpers alike (strong helpers were blocked only 0.6 percentage points more often), and a parent's own offspring made up about 12% of its neighbours in both groups. With grid-wide dispersal, none of the measured opportunities was blocked. So, locally, space was often short for strong and weak helpers alike, not specifically around helpers; the idea that helpers' own young fill the space around them is not supported in size. Why care spread further under grid-wide dispersal remains open.

**Mitteldorf and Wilson (2000): the empty-site model the ladder planned to use.** The ladder's original plan named this model, which the site replicates as [Spatial Altruism](/evolved-cooperation/spatial-altruism), as its template for a variable population with empty sites, and as a fallback base; neither use happened, because the later empty-space attempts (R5a to R8 and the 2026-11-24 restart) were built on the site's own engine. The harsh demo setting on its site page (not a setting from the paper) looked like the closest result the site had to what step 5 is missing. Each altruist pays a cost and shares a benefit with the five sites of its neighbourhood, itself included; at the settings of the page's main run (benefit 0.468, cost 0.156, so a benefit-to-cost ratio of 3) its own share, 0.094, is below its cost, so this is costly help in the sense of the definition, not weak altruism. Empty sites compete in the local birth lottery (`harshness` 0.96, `disease` 0.213), and in that run altruists, after losing ground early, became the majority of the remaining occupied sites (from 37.5% at step 25 to 59.4% at step 100); with either `harshness` or `disease` set to 0, altruists were extinct by step 200. It does not count as a step: it is one seeded run that starts with 39% altruists rather than rare, relatedness is not tracked, there is no control that removes it, and the occupied population shrinks as the empty space spreads. A separate culling sweep on the same page (5 runs per setting) found regions where altruists and selfish individuals coexist, but was not designed as a test of this definition. It was therefore tested against the definition on 2026-11-25, with a pre-registered design ([results](https://github.com/doesburg11/EvolvedCooperation/blob/main/ecological_models/spatial_altruism/RESULTS_KIN_TEST.md)). A screen first ran all-altruist and all-selfish populations on their own: at the setting of the page's main run (harshness 0.96, disease 0.213; a site demo setting, not taken from the paper, which tested harshness 0.8 to 0.95 at settings where all-selfish populations persist) all 10 all-selfish populations went extinct, so there altruism can win simply by being the only kind of population that survives; the test ran at 0.9, the closest setting where both kinds persist. There, costly help starting rare (5% of sites, scattered) reached the endpoint (altruists at least 60% of the occupied sites) in 0 of 50 runs, and altruists were essentially gone in 49 of them, although empty space was present throughout and occupied neighbours were strongly clustered by descent. Help that pays for itself (benefit 1.0) spread in all 50 runs at the same setting. This does not contradict the paper: the tested benefit (0.468) was below the stalemate benefit the paper's Table 2 implies for that cost (roughly 0.59, interpolated, at disease 0.2), and the test also differs from the paper's runs in disease (0.213) and in its rare start. At the demo setting, from a rare start, 42 of 50 populations went extinct and the 8 survivors were entirely altruist. So in this model costly help did not spread from rare where selfish populations can persist; the row is marked ✗. These results hold for this model and these settings (two fixed types, no mutation, 1,000 steps).

### Step 5 in empty-site islands (2026-11-25)

A theory screen of [Alizon and Taylor (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1558-5646.2008.00369.x) ([note](https://github.com/doesburg11/EvolvedCooperation/blob/main/ecological_models/nowak_mechanisms/kin_selection/ladder/theory/THEORY_SCREEN_ALIZON_TAYLOR.md)) re-derived their model's main figure from the paper's appendix and pointed to a condition the lattice attempts did not meet. In their model, patches of 10 sites hold breeders or empty sites, deaths do not depend on the trait, and every offspring competes for the empty sites, so a patch with more fecund breeders fills its gaps faster; there, a fecundity gift to patch-mates carries almost no kin-competition cost. On the lattice, an offspring could only take a free site next to its parent, and in the step-5 restart (a descriptive measure) 39% of individuals had no free neighbour at 86% occupancy, so births were limited by space rather than fecundity. That resembles the case in which the authors say extra fecundity given to one breeder mostly lowers the chances of the others; whether it caused the lattice failures was not tested. The paper's population rule was then ported as an individual-based model and tested with a pre-registered design ([pre-registration](https://github.com/doesburg11/EvolvedCooperation/blob/main/ecological_models/nowak_mechanisms/kin_selection/ladder/STEP5_EMPTY_SITES.md), [results](https://github.com/doesburg11/EvolvedCooperation/blob/main/ecological_models/nowak_mechanisms/kin_selection/ladder/RESULTS.md)). A first stage checked the port against the theory without helping: patch-type distribution, occupancy (0.534 against 0.535) and patch relatedness (0.146 to 0.148 against 0.147) all matched.

### Step 5 among non-offspring relatives (2026-11-26)

A theory screen ([note](https://github.com/doesburg11/EvolvedCooperation/blob/main/ecological_models/nowak_mechanisms/kin_selection/ladder/theory/THEORY_SCREEN_NO_OFFSPRING.md)) measured why the arm that excluded own offspring fell short. Own offspring are only 2 to 7% of a helper's patch-mates but carry about a third of its relatedness to them, so at benefit-to-cost 14 the push towards more helping was much weaker and stopped near a helping level of 0.8. At benefit-to-cost 28 the theory predicts spread at every level of helping. A new pre-registered test ([plan](https://github.com/doesburg11/EvolvedCooperation/blob/main/ecological_models/nowak_mechanisms/kin_selection/ladder/STEP5_NONOFFSPRING.md), [results](https://github.com/doesburg11/EvolvedCooperation/blob/main/ecological_models/nowak_mechanisms/kin_selection/ladder/RESULTS.md)) used that ratio, fresh seeds and a new control: the same gift goes into a pool shared by breeders in *other* patches with the same number of breeders, so the gift per breeder in each patch size stays the same while recipients are no more related than random breeders elsewhere (measured: relatedness along the gift routes 0.066 in the test arm, 0.0001 in the control).

## External check: Kanwal and Gardner (2022)

**Why it was run.** Step 5 failed once births needed free space, possibly because relatives born close together compete for the same space (kin competition). This is the classic obstacle: Taylor (1992) showed that when offspring disperse with a fixed probability, the benefit of living among relatives is exactly cancelled by competing with them, so population viscosity alone does not favour altruism. [Kanwal and Gardner (2022)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8905158/) argue that the cancellation breaks when dispersal depends on density: juveniles leave crowded patches more often, which relieves competition among relatives. To check that claim before building on it, their own simulation code ([jkanwal/viscosity-promotes-altruism](https://github.com/jkanwal/viscosity-promotes-altruism)) was run unchanged on their full grid, with the criteria written down before the runs (2026-11-21).

**The model.** 50,000 breeders live on patches of $n$ breeders. Each breeder has 100 offspring that copy its genes, apart from rare mutations. A juvenile's helping trait $z$ lowers its own survival, and the patch average of $z$ raises the survival of every juvenile on the patch. Each patch also draws a random survival factor, so the number of surviving juveniles differs between patches. Each juvenile then disperses with a probability that depends on how many juveniles survived on its patch, through an evolving rule, and pays a dispersal cost $c$ (the chance of dying on the way). Finally, each patch gets $n$ new breeders, drawn from the juveniles present (with repeats if there are fewer than $n$; an empty patch is refilled with new random individuals). The measure is the paper's *potential for altruism* $A$, computed as $A = z/(1-z)$ and averaged over the last 1,000 of 20,000 generations, as in the authors' analysis notebook (`data_analysis_and_figs.ipynb`). Under Taylor's cancellation, $A = 1/n$; the paper predicts $A$ above $1/n$ and rising with $c$. Grid: $n \in \{1, 2, 3, 5, 10\}$ × $c \in \{0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8\}$, one run per cell (25 runs, about 3 days each).

**Result: all criteria set in advance were met.** All 20 runs with $n > 1$ have $A$ above Taylor's $1/n$. The mixed-effects regression of $A$ on the realised dispersal rate from the authors' analysis notebook gives a slope of −0.177 (the notebook's published output: −0.178; the paper's text reports no slope). The permutation test gives $p = 10^{-5}$, and $p = 0.001$ in the notebook's own variant, which shuffles the median-based $A$ (the paper reports $p < 0.001$ from $10^5$ permutations). Beyond the registered test, $A$ rises with $c$ at every $n > 1$; it tracks the analytic curve closely for $n = 2$ and 3 but falls below it at larger $n$ and higher $c$ (for example 0.33 against 0.47 at $n = 10$, $c = 0.8$), while staying well above $1/n$.

**What the replication does and does not show.** It is an independent check on a published result: in the authors' model, with viscosity and evolving density-dependent dispersal, costly help to patch-mates (the sub-case *among non-offspring relatives*) is kept above Taylor's cancellation value. These runs started the helping trait at random values and included neither a fixed-dispersal control nor a control that removes relatedness; the test below adds a start with no helping and both controls. The number of breeders is fixed ($n$ per patch), so the model belongs to the first block of Display 1, as a reference row, not a numbered step. What it suggests for step 5 is a concrete ingredient to try: let offspring leave crowded neighbourhoods more often than sparse ones. The step-5 restart (2026-11-24) tried this only loosely, in one exploratory arm: offspring with no free neighbouring site moved to a random empty site anywhere (surviving the move with probability 0.8), a fixed rule rather than the evolving density-dependent dispersal of Kanwal and Gardner. Care reached the endpoint in 8 of 20 runs there, against 4 of 20 with local births only (p = 0.15, not significant). So the ingredient has been tried, loosely, with an inconclusive result; a step-5 test with evolving density-dependent dispersal has not been run.

**Test from no helping, with controls (pre-registered, 2026-11-26).** To place the model against the definition, a port of the authors' code to a faster language, first validated against their runs on all 25 cells, was run from no helping ($z = 0$ everywhere, so helping enters only by mutation) at dispersal cost $c = 0.8$, with $n = 2$ and $n = 5$ breeders per patch, 10 seeds each, in three arms: the authors' model (T); a control in which all juveniles are shuffled across patches before helping, which breaks up patch-level kin clustering (X); and fixed, density-independent dispersal, Taylor's setting (F) ([results](https://github.com/doesburg11/EvolvedCooperation/blob/main/ecological_models/density_dependent_dispersal/RESULTS_KIN_TEST.md)).
